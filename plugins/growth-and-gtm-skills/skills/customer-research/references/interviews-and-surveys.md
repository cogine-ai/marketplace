# Interviews and Surveys

Use primary research when you need evidence only a customer can provide.
Read the existing [extraction and confidence framework](../SKILL.md#extraction-framework) before designing the study.

## Plan to challenge an assumption

Name the decision, the assumption behind it, and what answer would change your mind. Write questions that could disprove the assumption. Keep the conversation natural, explain its purpose honestly, and avoid pitching or defending the product.

## Recruit deliberately

- For learning why customers succeed, recruit the customers you want more of: retained accounts, strong value realization, and relevant buying journeys. Ask sales/support for referrals and end interviews by asking who else has a relevant experience.
- For churn or purchase barriers, include the relevant churned/lost cohort instead of drawing conclusions from happy customers alone.
- Choose sample size and any incentive for the segments, question, budget, and remaining uncertainty. Record who responded and who did not; a fixed number of interviews does not establish saturation for every segment.
- Draft outreach with the purpose, requested time, incentive if approved, and a concrete next step. Do not send it or promise an incentive without authorization.

## Interview for observed behavior

Start with a recent real episode: what triggered the need, what the person tried, alternatives considered, what happened, and how they judged the result. Ask for examples before opinions about hypothetical features.

Follow a useful answer with “Why did that matter?”, “What happened next?”, or “Can you show an example?” until the underlying job or constraint is clear; do not mechanically demand five answers. Probe both pains and things people value enough to miss. Capture their words and distinguish direct quotes, observed behavior, and your interpretation.

## Design a short survey

Use open questions to discover language and reasons. Use multiple-choice questions when the options are justified, and allow responses outside them. Avoid questions that suggest the preferred answer.

For a product-market-fit pulse, ask how respondents would feel if they could no longer use the product: very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, not disappointed, or no longer using it. Follow with the main benefit, who would benefit most, and what could improve.

Report the eligible cohort, response count/rate, survey date, and segment differences. Pair responses with actual retention and behavior. A “very disappointed” percentage by itself is not proof of product-market fit, and a benchmark from another company is not a release threshold.

## Synthesize and label the evidence

Apply Mode 1's jobs, pains, triggers, outcomes, language, alternatives, and confidence rules. Compare contradictions across interviews, surveys, support material, and [public sources](source-guides.md). First-party evidence still needs a relevant sample; it is not automatically high confidence. Keep the research plan separate from interviews actually conducted and responses actually received.
